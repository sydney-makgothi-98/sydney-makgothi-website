01 · CONTEXT

A wing is a spring, and air can feed it energy

A large airliner wing is long, light and flexible. In flight it bends and twists under the air loads, and those deflections change the loads in turn. Two kinds of instability come out of that feedback. Divergence is static: the twist raises the lift, the extra lift twists the wing further, and above a certain speed the wing's torsional stiffness can no longer hold it. Flutter is dynamic: the bending and twisting motions couple so that each cycle the air does a little more work on the wing than the structure can dissipate, and a small disturbance grows into a violent oscillation. Both end in structural failure, so an aircraft has to be shown to be free of both, with a margin, across everything it can fly.

For a transport aircraft such as the Airbus A380 the usual requirement is that the aircraft be demonstrably free of flutter and divergence up to its dive speed VD with a margin of about 15 % in speed beyond it. Showing that is a three-part job: a structural model checked against a ground vibration test, an aeroelastic calculation of the flutter and divergence speeds, and then flight flutter testing, in which the aircraft is flown in steps up to VD while its modes are watched. This page shows the principle of that process on the smallest model that still flutters. It uses no data from any specific aircraft: the numbers are illustrative, chosen so that the behaviour is clear.

02 · THEORY

The typical section

Take one slice of the wing and give it two degrees of freedom: plunge h, the up and down motion, and pitch α, the twist about the elastic axis. The slice is held by a plunge spring Kh and a torsion spring Kα. The centre of gravity sits a distance xαb behind the elastic axis, which couples the two motions through inertia. The aerodynamic centre sits a distance ec ahead of the elastic axis, which couples them through the air. With quasi-steady aerodynamics the lift is $$L=q\,c\,C_{L\alpha}\Big(\alpha+\frac{\dot h}{V}+\big(\tfrac12-e\big)\frac{c\,\dot\alpha}{V}\Big),\qquad q=\tfrac12\rho V^{2}$$ and the equations of motion, per unit span, are

$$\begin{aligned}m\ddot h+S\ddot\alpha+K_h h&=-L\\ S\ddot h+I_\alpha\ddot\alpha+K_\alpha\alpha&=L\,e\,c+M_{\dot\alpha}\end{aligned}$$

where S = m xα b and Mα̇ is the thin-aerofoil pitch-damping moment. Written as a first-order system and solved for its eigenvalues λ at each airspeed, the answer is read off directly: for a complex pair λ = σ ± iω, the frequency is ω/2π and the damping ratio is −σ/|λ|. A positive damping ratio means that disturbances die away, and a negative one means they grow. Flutter is the speed at which one mode's damping crosses zero.

Divergence has a closed form

Set the time derivatives to zero. The lift is then a pure twist-lift, and the moment L e c softens the torsion spring: the effective torsional stiffness is Kα − q c² e CLα. Divergence is the dynamic pressure at which this reaches zero:

$$q_D=\frac{K_\alpha}{c^{2}\,e\,C_{L\alpha}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad V_{\text{div}}=\sqrt{\frac{2K_\alpha}{\rho\,c^{2}\,e\,C_{L\alpha}}}$$

Chart 03 below plots the effective stiffness collapsing towards zero as speed grows. Divergence depends only on the torsional stiffness and where the aerodynamic centre sits. Flutter also depends on the mass distribution, which is why moving the centre of gravity forward (mass balancing) is one of the classic cures.

03 · THE DIVE TEST

Reading the two graphs

In the stage above, each coloured dot is what a flight test would record at one test speed: the frequency and damping of the two modes, found by exciting the wing and watching the response decay, with some scatter because real measurements are never clean. The faint dashed curves are the model's prediction, which a real test would be compared against but would not know in advance for the aircraft itself.

  • Frequencies approach each other. The plunge-like mode (blue) rises with speed and the pitch-like mode (orange) falls, until they come together. That coalescence is the signature of coupled-mode flutter.
  • Damping gives the warning. The pitch-like mode loses damping as the speed rises and trends towards zero. The amber line is a straight-line fit to the last few points, and where it meets zero is the predicted flutter speed. The test is flown upward in steps of speed, and the flight is stopped or the next step withheld when the trend is too steep.
  • The margin. The flutter speed must lie beyond 1.15 VD. The result below says whether it does with the current wing.

Try it. Lower the torsion frequency to about 7 Hz, or move the centre of gravity aft, and the flutter speed falls inside the dive envelope: the clearance turns to FAIL and the wing section in the stage starts to grow in amplitude and the status label changes to flutter. Raise the torsion frequency and the margin returns. Stiffening the wing, mass-balancing it, or reducing VD are the three levers, and in a real programme each costs weight, range or performance.

Limits of the model. A real wing has many modes, not two. Its aerodynamics are unsteady and depend on Mach number, computed with methods such as the doublet-lattice method and checked by the p-k solution of the flutter equations. The stiffness and mass come from a finite-element model validated by a ground vibration test. The two-degree-of-freedom model here keeps only the physics that sets the trend, which is why its numbers should be read as illustrative.
