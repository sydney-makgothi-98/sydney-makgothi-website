01 · CONTEXT

A floor, idealised as a beam on springs

A floor in a building looks like a slab resting on columns and walls, but its stiffness is not concentrated at those points: the supporting structure below it deflects too, and the load path is continuous. A useful idealisation, and the one used here, treats a strip of the floor as a platform supported by an infinite number of linear springs spread continuously from one end to the other. Each spring pushes back in proportion to how far the floor above it has moved down. The platform is a beam of flexural rigidity EI and span L, held at its two ends, and loaded by the dead load of the floor (and, optionally, a heavy machine).

The goal is the displacement w(x) of the floor, from which the bending moments, the spring reactions and the serviceability (is the floor too bendy?) follow. The beam-on-springs equation could be solved directly, and for this one loading it has an exact answer. The reason to use an energy method instead is generality: the same procedure works for loads, supports and stiffness distributions that have no closed-form solution, and it turns a differential equation into a small matrix problem.

Normalising the length. To make the polynomials simple and the matrices pure numbers, the span is scaled to run from 0 to 1: ξ = x / L. Every integral over the floor then runs from ξ = 0 to 1, and L appears only as a factor in front.

02 · THEORY

The energy functional

Rayleigh–Ritz is the principle of minimum total potential energy made computable. Of all the displaced shapes that fit the supports, the true one makes the total potential energy Π stationary. Three energies make up Π:

$$\Pi = U_b + U_s - W,\qquad U_b=\frac12\int_0^L EI\,(w'')^2\,dx,\qquad U_s=\frac12\int_0^L k\,w^2\,dx,\qquad W=\int_0^L q\,w\,dx + P\,w(x_p)$$

Ub is the strain energy stored in bending (curvature squared, times the rigidity); Us is the energy stored in the continuous springs (each stores ½kw² per unit length); W is the work done by the loads as the floor moves down. Substituting x = Lξ (so that w″ = wξξ/L²) turns the three into dimensionless integrals:

$$U_b=\frac{EI}{2L^3}\int_0^1 (w_{\xi\xi})^2\,d\xi,\qquad U_s=\frac{kL}{2}\int_0^1 w^2\,d\xi,\qquad W=qL\int_0^1 w\,d\xi + P\,w(\xi_p)$$

The polynomial series

The unknown shape is approximated by a sum of known polynomials with unknown coefficients. Each polynomial must satisfy the geometric boundary conditions (zero displacement at both ends) and nothing else is required:

$$w(\xi)\approx\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_i\,\phi_i(\xi),\qquad \phi_i(\xi)=\xi^{\,i}(1-\xi):\quad \phi_1=\xi-\xi^2,\ \ \phi_2=\xi^2-\xi^3,\ \ \phi_3=\xi^3-\xi^4,\ \dots$$

The stiffness matrix

Putting the series into Π makes it a quadratic function of the coefficients. Setting ∂Π/∂ai = 0 for each i gives N linear equations, K a = F, with

$$K_{ij}=\frac{EI}{L^3}\int_0^1\phi_i''\,\phi_j''\,d\xi+kL\int_0^1\phi_i\,\phi_j\,d\xi,\qquad F_i=qL\int_0^1\phi_i\,d\xi+P\,\phi_i(\xi_p)$$

Because the basis functions are monomials, every integral can be done exactly. With ∫φi dξ = 1/((i+1)(i+2)) and ∫φiφj dξ = 1/(i+j+1) − 2/(i+j+2) + 1/(i+j+3), the whole matrix is assembled from fractions. The first term is the beam's stiffness, the second the springs'; the uniform load's vector is the same for every floor. The first, one-term, solution is simply a1 = (qL/6) / (4EI/L³ + kL/30).

04 · WHY IT WORKS

Upper bounds, convergence and a note on the series

The Ritz displacement is always stiffer than the truth: restricting the shape to a family of polynomials can only add constraints, so the computed energy is an upper bound on the true minimum and the computed mid-span deflection approaches the exact value from below as terms are added. In the numbers above, N = 1 gives 4.87 mm against an exact 5.40 mm, N = 3 gives 5.42 mm, and N = 5 reproduces it to four digits. A symmetric load excites only symmetric shapes, which is why terms improve the answer in pairs. Bending moments converge more slowly than displacements, because they involve a second derivative of the series; the moment chart is the one to watch when choosing N.

The method is also honest about the assumptions behind it: the floor strip is one-dimensional, the springs are linear and independent (a Winkler bed), the beam is elastic and slender, and the supports are idealised as pins. The value of the idealisation is that each assumption is explicit and can be relaxed: a varying EI or k only changes the integrals, and a different support condition only changes the basis functions.

The default values (a 6 m span, a 200 mm reinforced concrete strip with EI = 20 MN·m², 15 kN/m of load) are a representative office floor chosen for the demonstration, so that the sliders can move them across a realistic range.
