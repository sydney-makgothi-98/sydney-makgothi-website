01 · THE BRIEF

One litre of cylinder, one megawatt of engine

The target is the engine of the Koenigsegg One:1: a twin-turbocharged 5.0-litre V8, named for its one megawatt of power for one tonne of car. The manufacturer's published figures (5 065 cm³ from a 92 mm bore and 95.25 mm stroke, a 9.0:1 compression ratio, 1 360 hp at 7 500 rpm, 1 371 N·m at 6 000 rpm, an 8 250 rpm limiter) are the starting constraints. Everything else, the rod length, the boost, the masses, is a design choice, and this page is the machinery for making those choices: change a dimension and see the pressure, the loads and the torque change with it.

02 · THERMODYNAMIC CYCLE

The Otto cycle, and why the real engine falls short of it

An engine converts the chemical energy of fuel into work by taking a charge of air and fuel round a cycle: compress it, burn it, let it expand against the piston, throw it away. The simplest model that captures the physics is the air-standard Otto cycle, in which a fixed mass of ideal gas goes through four processes:

  • 1 to 2, isentropic compression from bottom dead centre to top dead centre: no heat, so p Vγ is constant and T2 = T1 rcγ−1, where rc = Vmax/Vmin.
  • 2 to 3, heat added at constant volume: the burn is taken as instantaneous at TDC, Qin = m cv(T3 − T2).
  • 3 to 4, isentropic expansion: the power stroke, T4 = T3/rcγ−1.
  • 4 to 1, heat rejected at constant volume as the exhaust opens, Qout = m cv(T4 − T1).

The thermal efficiency is the work out over the heat in, and because the two isentropes share the same temperature ratio, everything cancels but the compression ratio:

$$\eta=\frac{Q_{in}-Q_{out}}{Q_{in}}=1-\frac{T_4-T_1}{T_3-T_2}=1-\frac{T_1}{T_2}=1-r_c^{\,1-\gamma}$$

Raising the compression ratio raises the efficiency, with diminishing returns, as the curves below show. The ratio is capped by knock, the spontaneous detonation of the unburned charge ahead of the flame, which depends on the pressure and temperature at the end of compression: a boosted engine already arrives at TDC with a hot, dense charge, so a turbocharged engine such as this one runs a modest 9.0:1 and uses the boost, not the ratio, to make its power. Real efficiency is also lower than the ideal because γ falls from 1.4 for cold air to nearer 1.3 for hot burnt gas (the lower curves), because the burn takes time and is not at constant volume, and because heat is lost to the walls.

03 · TRANSIENT ANALYSIS

Deriving the pressure trace, step by step

The ideal cycle gives a number. To size a cylinder, a piston and a block, the pressure is needed at every crank angle, because that is what loads the parts. The tool for that is the first law applied to the cylinder contents as a closed system between inlet valve closing and exhaust valve opening, advanced in small crank-angle steps. The derivation takes five steps.

Step 1, energy balance. The internal energy U of the charge changes by the heat released by combustion, less the heat lost to the walls, less the work done on the piston:

$$dU=\delta Q_b-\delta Q_w-p\,dV,\qquad U=m\,c_v\,T$$

Step 2, the gas law. With p V = m R T and cv = R/(γ−1), the internal energy is U = pV/(γ−1). Differentiating that and substituting into step 1 gives $$\frac{p\,dV+V\,dp}{\gamma-1}=\delta Q-p\,dV\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dp}{d\theta}=\frac{\gamma-1}{V}\frac{dQ}{d\theta}-\gamma\,\frac{p}{V}\frac{dV}{d\theta},\qquad \delta Q=\delta Q_b-\delta Q_w$$

This is the whole model in one line. The first term is the pressure rise from burning fuel. The second is the cylinder volume changing as the piston moves: it is negative on compression, so it raises the pressure, and positive on expansion, so it lowers it.

Step 3, the heat release. Fuel does not burn all at once. A Wiebe function describes the burned mass fraction, an S-shaped curve that starts slowly, accelerates through the middle of the flame and tails off:

$$x_b(\theta)=1-\exp\!\left[-a\left(\frac{\theta-\theta_0}{\Delta\theta}\right)^{m+1}\right],\qquad \frac{dQ_b}{d\theta}=Q_{tot}\,\frac{dx_b}{d\theta}$$

where θ0 is the spark timing, Δθ the burn duration, and a and m shape the curve. The total heat Qtot is the fuel mass times its heating value times a combustion efficiency, and the fuel mass is the charge mass divided by the air-fuel ratio plus one.

Step 4, the volume. V(θ) is the clearance volume plus the piston area times its travel, and the travel is the slider-crank displacement derived in the next section. This is the link between the mechanism and the thermodynamics: change the rod length and the pressure trace changes with it.

Step 5, march. Start at bottom dead centre with the intake pressure and temperature, step in half-degree increments, update p from step 2 and the work as W = ∑ p ΔV, and read off the indicated mean effective pressure imep = W/Vd. Friction is then subtracted with the Chen–Flynn correlation to give brake torque.

A single zone with uniform temperature, a fixed γ ramp and a lumped 10 % heat loss is the simplest honest model. It trades away flame propagation, the end-gas that causes knock and the temperature gradients across the chamber, which a multi-zone or CFD model would resolve.

04 · ABSOLUTE MOTION

The piston and connecting rod as a linkage

To find what the piston and rod must withstand, the crank, rod and piston are modelled as a linkage of three moving bodies: a crank turning about the fixed point O, a connecting rod joining the crank pin A to the piston pin B, and a piston that can only slide along the cylinder axis. The method is absolute motion analysis: write the position of each moving point in a fixed frame at O, and differentiate. No relative-velocity diagrams are needed, and the result is exact.

Position. Put y along the cylinder axis. The pin A goes round a circle of radius r, so rA = r(sinθ, cosθ), measured from the crank angle θ from top dead centre. The rod of length l joins A to B, so B is at rB = rA + rB/A with rB/A = l(−sinψ, −cosψ) where ψ is the rod angle. The piston cannot leave the axis, so the x component of rB is zero:

$$0=r\sin\theta-l\sin\psi\;\Longrightarrow\;\sin\psi=\lambda\sin\theta,\qquad y_B=r\cos\theta+l\cos\psi=r\cos\theta+\sqrt{l^2-r^2\sin^2\theta}$$

Measuring the travel x from top dead centre (x = l + r − yB) gives the displacement equation used throughout this page:

$$x(\theta)=r\,(1-\cos\theta)+l-\sqrt{l^{2}-r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta},\qquad \lambda=\frac rl$$
The linkage and its displacement
live: the crank angle follows the engine in the stage

Differentiate once. Every point moves because the crank turns at a steady ω = dθ/dt, so each time derivative is a derivative with respect to θ multiplied by ω. The velocity of the piston is $$v=\dot x=\omega\,r\sin\theta\left[1+\frac{r\cos\theta}{\sqrt{l^{2}-r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}}\right]$$

Differentiate twice. Differentiating again, and keeping ω constant, gives the acceleration, which is exactly what the page uses to calculate the loads:

$$a=\ddot x=\omega^{2}\left[\,r\cos\theta+\frac{r^{2}(\cos^{2}\theta-\sin^{2}\theta)}{\sqrt{l^{2}-r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}}+\frac{r^{4}\sin^{2}\theta\cos^{2}\theta}{\left(l^{2}-r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\right)^{3/2}}\right]$$

Expanding the root as a binomial series, √(l²−r²sin²θ) ≈ l(1 − λ²sin²θ/2), turns the displacement into r[(1−cosθ) + (λ/4)(1−cos2θ)] and the acceleration into the compact form $$a\approx r\omega^{2}\left(\cos\theta+\lambda\cos2\theta\right)$$

which is a first harmonic of the crank speed and a second harmonic of twice that speed: the second harmonic is the reason a single-plane four-cylinder engine shakes at twice engine speed, and why a rod ratio matters.

The acceleration curve, and what to read from it

Piston acceleration over one revolution
the dashed line follows the crank; the numbers use the speed set in the stage
  • Top dead centre is the maximum. aTDC = rω²(1+λ). The piston stops and turns round, and the crank must supply the acceleration, so this is the instant of greatest load on the pin, the rod and its bolts. It acts to pull the piston away from the crown, so on the exhaust and intake TDC, where the gas pressure is low, it is a pure tension load on the rod.
  • The zero crossing is the fastest point. Where the acceleration is zero the velocity is at its maximum, about 75° either side of TDC.
  • Bottom dead centre has a dimple. With a rod ratio above one quarter the acceleration at BDC, −rω²(1−λ), is not the deepest: it sits between two slightly deeper minima, which is the signature of the second harmonic.

At the car's top speed

At top speed the engine is taken to be at its 8 250 rpm limiter in top gear (an assumption: the car's gearing is not public). The reciprocating inertia is the piston plus the share of the rod that moves with it, and the numbers below use this design's masses.

05 · FORCES AND TORQUE

What the piston feels, and what the crank gets

The gas pushes the piston down with p·A. Its own mass fights back: the reciprocating inertia force is mra, which pulls the piston away from the crank near top dead centre and grows with the square of engine speed. Chart 04 draws gas, inertia and their sum. At the redline the inertia force alone is tens of kilonewtons, which is why piston and rod mass are minimised. The net force turns into torque on the crank through the rod's geometry:

$$T(\theta)=F_{net}\,r\,\frac{\sin(\theta+\psi)}{\cos\psi},\qquad bmep=imep-fmep,\qquad T=\frac{bmep\,V_d}{4\pi},\qquad P=T\omega$$

One cylinder delivers its torque in a single violent pulse every two revolutions. Eight cylinders overlap, and chart 05 shows how the ripple falls from huge to modest. Here the cylinders fire every 90°. The flywheel fills in the rest. Friction is estimated with the Chen–Flynn correlation, which charges the engine for peak pressure and piston speed.

06 · OPTIMISING THE DIMENSIONS

What each slider trades away

  • Bore (with displacement held). A bigger bore means a shorter stroke, so a lower piston speed at the same rpm and room for larger valves; but the flame has further to travel and the combustion chamber is less compact.
  • Rod length. Longer rods lower the side thrust and the piston's time at the top, but lengthen the engine and add mass; the practical window is a rod ratio of 0.28 to 0.33.
  • Compression ratio. Higher gives more efficiency and more peak pressure; on boost it is capped by knock.
  • Boost. Power rises almost linearly with the manifold pressure and so does peak cylinder pressure, which sets the strength of the block, head studs, pistons and crank.
  • Speed. Power rises with rpm, torque falls slowly as breathing worsens and friction grows, and the inertia force grows with the square: the redline is where the rods and bearings run out of margin.

The model is a design-study tool: a single-zone first-law cycle with a Wiebe burn, an exact slider-crank, and a friction correlation. Its boost, masses and rod length are chosen here so that the output lands on the published figures (within about five percent on torque), and every number on the page changes together when a slider moves.
