CASE 01 — SUPERSONIC INTAKE

Intake design for a drone flying at Mach 3.3

FREESTREAMM∞ = 3.3
REGIMESupersonic
KEY RELATIONθ–β–M
METRICp₀ recovery

A jet engine can't swallow air at Mach 3.3. The intake has to slow the flow to subsonic speed before it reaches the compressor face, and every shock it uses to do that destroys some total pressure. The design problem is to decelerate the flow with as little loss as possible.

A single normal shock at Mach 3.3 would do the job, but it is very wasteful. Instead, the intake uses a series of compression ramps. Each ramp turns the flow through a small angle and creates a weak oblique shock. A terminal normal shock at the cowl lip then takes the already-slowed flow to subsonic speed. Several weak shocks lose much less total pressure than one strong one.

Oblique shock relations

Each ramp's shock angle \(\beta\) follows from the upstream Mach number and the ramp deflection angle \(\theta\) through the θ–β–M relation:

θ–β–M RELATION $$\tan\theta = 2\cot\beta\,\frac{M_1^2\sin^2\beta - 1}{M_1^2(\gamma + \cos 2\beta) + 2}$$

The normal component of the Mach number, \(M_{n1} = M_1\sin\beta\), then sets the jump in pressure and the total pressure loss across that shock:

TOTAL PRESSURE RATIO $$\frac{p_{0,2}}{p_{0,1}} = \left[\frac{(\gamma+1)M_{n1}^2}{(\gamma-1)M_{n1}^2+2}\right]^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}} \left[\frac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma M_{n1}^2-(\gamma-1)}\right]^{\frac{1}{\gamma-1}}$$

The overall intake recovery is the product of the ratios across every shock in the system, and it is usually compared against the military standard MIL-E-5008B:

MIL-E-5008B REFERENCE $$\eta_{\text{MIL}} = 1 - 0.075\,(M_\infty - 1)^{1.35}, \qquad 1 < M_\infty < 5$$

CASE 02 — ROCKET EXHAUST

Modelling an underexpanded rocket slot jet

NOZZLE2D slot
EXPANSIONpe > pa
KEY RELATIONθ–β–M
FEATUREShock cells

When a rocket nozzle's exit pressure is higher than the surrounding air, the exhaust leaves underexpanded: it keeps expanding after it leaves the nozzle. That sets up a repeating pattern of expansion fans and shocks in the plume, the "shock diamonds" you can see behind a rocket engine.

How the shock cells form

  1. Expansion at the lip. At the nozzle exit the jet is still above ambient pressure, so a Prandtl–Meyer expansion fan forms at each lip and turns the jet boundary outwards.
  2. Reflection at the boundary. The jet boundary is a constant-pressure surface. The expansion waves reflect from it as compression waves, which bend the flow back towards the centreline.
  3. Coalescence into a shock. The compression waves converge into an oblique (intercepting) shock. Its angle follows from the θ–β–M relation for the local Mach number and turning angle.
  4. Repeat. The shocks cross at the centreline and reflect, over-compress the jet, and the cycle starts again. Mixing with the ambient air slowly damps each new cell.

The turning at the lip comes from the Prandtl–Meyer function. The flow expands from the exit Mach number \(M_e\) to the Mach number \(M_j\) that brings it down to ambient pressure:

PRANDTL–MEYER FUNCTION $$\nu(M) = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}}\tan^{-1}\sqrt{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma+1}(M^2-1)} - \tan^{-1}\sqrt{M^2-1}, \qquad \theta_{\text{lip}} = \nu(M_j) - \nu(M_e)$$

The compression that turns the boundary back through \(\theta_{\text{lip}}\) is then modelled as an oblique shock with the θ–β–M relation, which gives the shock angle and the jump in pressure across it.

CASE 03 — BOUNDARY LAYER ANALYSIS

Rolls-Royce Spirit of Innovation wing at Mach 0.45

FLIGHT SPEEDM = 0.45
REGIMESubsonic
EFFECTSCompressible
OUTPUTδ, Cf

The Spirit of Innovation is Rolls-Royce's all-electric speed-record aircraft. At Mach 0.45 it is well into the range where air can no longer be treated as incompressible, so this analysis looks at how the boundary layer on its wing grows and how compressibility changes skin friction and surface temperature.

The boundary layer starts laminar at the leading edge, becomes unstable and transitions to turbulence. The turbulent layer grows much faster and creates more skin friction drag. Where transition happens is set by the local Reynolds number:

BOUNDARY LAYER THICKNESS $$\delta_{\text{lam}} \approx \frac{5.0\,x}{\sqrt{Re_x}}, \qquad \delta_{\text{turb}} \approx \frac{0.37\,x}{Re_x^{1/5}}, \qquad Re_x = \frac{\rho U_\infty x}{\mu}$$

Accounting for compressibility

At Mach 0.45 the flow near the wall is heated by viscous friction, which changes the density and viscosity inside the boundary layer. Three corrections capture this:

  1. Recovery temperature. An insulated wall heats up towards the adiabatic wall temperature \(T_{aw} = T_\infty\left(1 + r\,\tfrac{\gamma-1}{2}M_\infty^2\right)\), with recovery factor \(r \approx Pr^{1/2}\) (laminar) or \(Pr^{1/3}\) (turbulent).
  2. Reference temperature. Eckert's method evaluates fluid properties at \(T^{*}/T_\infty = 1 + 0.032M_\infty^2 + 0.58\,(T_w/T_\infty - 1)\), so the incompressible skin-friction formulas can be reused.
  3. Pressure scaling. The Prandtl–Glauert factor \(1/\sqrt{1 - M_\infty^2}\) scales the incompressible pressure coefficients to compressible conditions.
