01 · THE PROBLEM

Stopping 3,175 kg that arrives at 168 m/s

The Shockwave is a 1985 Peterbilt tractor carrying three Pratt & Whitney J34-48 turbojets. Each makes about 3,000 lbf of thrust, 36,000 hp combined, and the truck holds a speed of 376 mph (168.2 m/s) at a mass near 7,000 lb (3,175 kg). At that speed the truck carries roughly 45 MJ of kinetic energy, and nothing in its wheel brakes can remove that safely before the end of a long dry lake.

This project, written up with Steven Sun for the KTS engineering blog in May 2023, was the design of the system that does it. The objective was a stop within 30 seconds with no more than 3.5 g sustained, because that is about what a driver can tolerate while still controlling the truck. The solution is staged: remove the energy where it is cheapest to remove, at the highest speed, using aerodynamic drag, and hand over to mechanical brakes only when the speed is low.

02 · THE ENGINE

A 1940s turbojet, three of them on one truck

What pushes the Shockwave is three Pratt & Whitney J34-48 turbojets, each making about 3,000 lbf of thrust. The J34 family dates from the late 1940s and is a single-shaft, axial-flow turbojet: air comes in through the inlet, an axial compressor squeezes it, fuel is burned in the combustor, and the hot gas first drives the turbine, which is on the same shaft as the compressor and powers it, and then leaves through the nozzle as a fast jet. The surplus momentum is the thrust. Nothing in it is complicated, and that is the point: it is a machine that makes its own compressed air, so it can run on little more than fuel.

  • Inlet. Collects the air and slows it a little, so that the compressor sees a smooth, steady stream.
  • Compressor. Rows of rotating and fixed blades pass the air along a narrowing annulus, raising its pressure and temperature stage by stage.
  • Combustor. Fuel is sprayed in and burned in the compressed air. This is where almost all the heat is added, and where the gas becomes hottest.
  • Turbine. Takes just enough energy out of the gas to drive the compressor. What is left is thrust.
  • Nozzle. Narrows to turn the remaining pressure into speed.

Why the start matters. A turbojet cannot start itself. Below about a sixth of its full speed the compressor pumps too little air for the turbine to keep it turning, so a starter has to spin the rotor first. Only then are fuel and the igniter switched on. The flame must take hold without the exhaust temperature running over its limit (a “hot start”), and the starter stays engaged until the turbine can carry the compressor alone. After that the engine accelerates, through idle, to full power. The animation shows that sequence for one engine.

03 · THREE STAGES

Drogue, reefed primary, brakes

  • Stage 1, drogue. An 8 ft Kevlar drogue is released at 350+ mph. Its job is to cut speed to roughly 250 mph at 1.5 to 2.0 g and to keep the truck straight. Kevlar is used because it tolerates the heat and the opening shock at that speed.
  • Stage 2, primary. At about 250 mph an 18 ft reinforced-nylon canopy takes over, targeting 80 mph at 2.5 to 3.0 g. It does not open at once. Dual-stage reefing holds the canopy partly closed by a line around its skirt: 40 % open for the first 2 seconds, 70 % for the next 3, fully open after 5 seconds. Without it the sudden jump in drag would be far more than the truck or the lines could take.
  • Stage 3, brakes. Below 80 mph the mechanical brakes finish the stop at under 2.0 g, with the canopy still pulling.

The reefing schedule is the difference between a controlled stop and a spike. Opened in a single bang, the primary would produce one violent jump in the g trace; held partly closed and let out in two steps, it produces the smooth rise seen in the deceleration chart further down. That is exactly why it was designed.

04 · THE MODEL

One equation drives the simulation

While the canopies are out, the truck is a point mass slowed by aerodynamic drag, rolling resistance and, later, the brakes:

$$m\,\frac{dv}{dt}=-\Big[\tfrac12\rho\,v^{2}\,(C_dA)(t)\;+\;F_{\text{brake}}\;+\;\mu_r\,m\,g\Big]$$

The drag area CdA is the whole design. A canopy of diameter d has area πd²/4 and a drag coefficient Cd; the simulation ramps CdA smoothly from zero as each canopy inflates, multiplies the primary by its reefing fraction in the first 5 seconds, and releases the brakes when the speed falls through 80 mph. Because the force goes with v², drag is strongest right at release and fades as the truck slows, which is why a parachute removes speed quickly at first and then needs the brakes to finish.

The blog describes computational fluid dynamics in ANSYS Fluent to examine canopy shape and wake. This page does not reproduce that CFD. It uses the one-equation model above, with drag coefficients assumed and tuned so that the drogue stage gives the 1.5 to 2.0 g the blog quotes. Treat it as a way to see how the design choices trade against each other, not as a replacement for the tests.

Where the model agrees with the tests, and where it does not

The distance agrees closely. The time and the peak do not: with the stage speeds and g ranges listed in the blog, a single-equation model stops the truck sooner than 24.7 s and never reaches a 3.2 g peak. The blog's own figures also do not fully agree with one another (a 24.7 s stop with 2.1 g sustained cannot also cover 4,200 ft on a smooth speed curve), so the real run was presumably more front-loaded than this model, with some shock loading at opening that a quasi-steady drag model leaves out. The sliders let you move toward the reported time (try a lower air density and a smaller primary), but the honest conclusion is that the reported run contains dynamics this model does not.

06 · MATERIALS AND SAFETY

What it is made of, and what keeps it safe

The drogue is Kevlar 49 for strength and heat resistance. The primary canopy is type IV ripstop nylon, light and tear-resistant, with Spectra suspension lines for strength per weight and a stainless reefing cable that does not stretch. Attachment points use titanium hardware. The blog reports a system mass of 187 lb, and testing of more than 50 deployments in which every one worked.

Safety comes from the staging itself. The drogue steadies the truck before the primary opens, reefing limits the opening load on the canopy and the driver, and the mechanical brakes are a second, independent means of stopping. The reported results are a 3.2 g peak, 2.1 g sustained, and a stop within the 30 second objective.

Source: KTS engineering blog, “Parachute Design for the Shockwave Jet Truck”, Gopolang Sydney Makgothi and Steven Sun, 28 May 2023. The reported figures above are theirs; the simulation is a separate model written for this page.
