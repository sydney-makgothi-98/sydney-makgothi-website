01 · THE IDEA

A pump adds angular momentum

Water enters the impeller's eye along the shaft and is thrown outwards by the blades. Between the eye and the rim its angular momentum rises from almost nothing to r₂cu2, and the torque that did it is the motor's. Euler's turbomachinery equation turns that statement into a head:

$$H_{th}=\frac{u_2\,c_{u2}}{g},\qquad u_2=\omega r_2,\qquad c_{u2}=\sigma u_2-\frac{c_{m2}}{\tan\beta_2},\qquad c_{m2}=\frac{Q}{\pi D_2 b_2\,\tau_2}$$

The first term is the tip speed, which is why head goes with the square of speed. The second is the cost of moving more water: more flow means a larger meridional velocity cm2 and, for a backward-curved blade (small β2), a smaller whirl velocity, so the head falls as flow rises. σ is the slip factor: the water in the channel does not follow the blade perfectly, so it leaves with less whirl than the blade geometry promises. This pump uses Wiesner's correlation, σ = 1 − √sinβ2 / z0.7.

02 · PUMP AND SYSTEM

Where the pump actually runs

A pump has no flow of its own. It runs where its curve meets the system curve: the static lift to the roof tank plus the friction in the irrigation main, which grows with the square of the flow, Hsys = Hstatic + K Q². Close the valve and K grows, the crossing slides up and to the left, efficiency falls: the pump is being throttled. The best efficiency point is where the water meets the blades most nearly without shock, and a pump left far from it suffers radial loads, recirculation and early bearing failure. Try the valve slider.

$$H(Q)=H_{th}-k_fQ^2-k_s\,(Q-Q_{sl})^2,\qquad \eta=\frac{\rho g Q H}{P_{shaft}}$$

The first loss is friction, rising with the square of the flow. The second is incidence loss, which is zero at the shockless flow Qsl where the water arrives exactly along the blade's leading edge and grows either side of it. Their sum is why the efficiency curve is a hill.

03 · BOUNDARY LAYER THEORY

What the blade does to the water next to it

Seen from the blade, the water is a free stream of relative speed w and the blade surface is a wall where the water must stop. The thin region in which the speed climbs from zero at the wall to w is the boundary layer. Its thickness sets the friction on the blade and how much of the passage the blade effectively blocks, so it feeds directly into how the impeller is sized.

Laminar layer. For steady flow along a flat plate with no pressure gradient, the boundary-layer equations reduce, through a similarity variable η = y√(w/νx), to a single ordinary differential equation, Blasius' equation. Its numerical solution gives the thickness where the speed reaches 99 % of w, the displacement thickness (how far the outer flow is pushed away from the wall) and the skin friction:

$$\delta=\frac{4.91\,x}{\sqrt{Re_x}},\qquad \delta^*=\frac{1.72\,x}{\sqrt{Re_x}},\qquad C_f=\frac{0.664}{\sqrt{Re_x}},\qquad Re_x=\frac{w\,x}{\nu}$$

The same results follow, to within 10 %, from the von Kármán momentum integral, which is the method that extends to other situations: the friction at the wall equals the rate at which momentum is lost in the layer, τw = ρ w² dθ/dx, and assuming a velocity profile (a parabola, u/w = 2η − η²) closes it and gives δ = 5.48 x/√Re.

Transition and the turbulent layer. Beyond a critical Reynolds number near 5 × 105 the layer becomes turbulent. A one-seventh power-law velocity profile then gives the commonly used results below. A turbulent layer is thicker and carries more friction than a laminar one at the same Reynolds number, but it resists separation better, which matters on the suction side of a blade where the pressure rises along the flow.

$$\delta_{turb}\approx\frac{0.37\,x}{Re_x^{1/5}},\qquad \delta^*_{turb}\approx\frac{0.046\,x}{Re_x^{1/5}},\qquad C_f\approx\frac{0.074}{Re_x^{1/5}}$$

Chart 05 in the stage draws both layers along the blade at the current flow with the transition marked. Raise the speed and the layer thins and transitions earlier. The blade section drawn there is a NACA 4-digit thickness form scaled to the real blade thickness, and the blade Reynolds number in the table above is the one to enter into XFLR5 when comparing candidate aerofoil sections for lift, drag and separation.

04 · SIZING THE PUMP

Using the boundary layer to size the passages

Three sizing decisions rest on the boundary layer, and the numbers below recompute from the pump above when the sliders move.

  • Blockage. The displacement thickness on both walls of a blade passage shrinks the area the water can really use. If the two layers together take more than about a tenth of the passage width, the meridional velocity cm2 (and so the head, through the Euler equation) is no longer the one the ideal geometry predicts. This sets how narrow the passages can be, and why a small pump cannot simply be a scaled-down large one: the layers do not scale down with it.
  • Friction loss. The wall shear Cf multiplied over the wetted blade area is the friction term kf in the head equation. Longer, more tightly packed blades give more guidance (and a better slip factor) but more friction, which is the trade behind the choice of blade count.
  • Surface finish. The viscous sublayer is thin. A roughness smaller than about 5 ν/uτ is hydraulically smooth and costs nothing, while a larger one sticks through the sublayer and raises friction. Compare the allowable roughness below with the 100 µm or more that is typical of a sand-cast surface, and it is clear why impeller passages are cleaned up or the casting process is chosen for finish.

05 · CAVITATION

Net positive suction head

Inside the eye the water speeds up and its pressure drops. If the pressure falls to the vapour pressure at the water temperature, vapour bubbles form and then collapse violently against the metal: cavitation. The pump needs a certain margin over the vapour pressure at its inlet, NPSHrequired, and the installation provides NPSHavailable:

$$NPSH_a=\frac{p_{atm}-p_v}{\rho g}-z_s-h_{f,s},\qquad NPSH_r\approx\lambda_c\frac{c_{m1}^2}{2g}+\lambda_w\frac{w_1^2}{2g}$$

Raise the suction lift or the speed (the second term grows with the square of both the flow and the speed) and chart 04 shows the margin eaten away and the red cavitation zone opening up from the right. It is why irrigation pumps sit as close to the sump as the layout allows.

06 · MATERIAL SCIENCE

Stiffness, strength and what the blade really has to survive

A solid resists being stretched because its atoms sit in a potential well: pull them apart and the bonds pull back. For small strains the force is proportional to the stretch, which is Hooke's law at the scale of the material, σ = Eε. Young's modulus E is the slope of the stress–strain line and measures how stiff the bonds are, so it is set by the type of bonding and barely changes with heat treatment or alloying. Yield strength is where the line ends and the material starts to deform permanently, and that is controlled by microstructure, which is why a heat-treated steel and an annealed one share a modulus and not a strength. Merit indices and the Ashby chart

When the aim is the lightest blade that is stiff enough, the right property is not E on its own. A blade is a thin plate loaded in bending, and the bending stiffness of a plate of thickness t is proportional to E t³. For a required stiffness the thickness scales as E−1/3, so the mass of a plate of fixed width and length scales as ρ/E1/3. The merit index for a stiff, light plate is therefore E1/3/ρ, and the best materials make it largest. The equivalent for a beam is E1/2/ρ, and for a tie loaded in tension E/ρ. On the Ashby chart below, with both axes logarithmic, each index is a straight guideline whose slope is 3, 2 or 1; sliding it up and to the left until it only touches one material picks the best. With stresses of a few megapascals against yield strengths in the hundreds, any of the metals would carry the load. What actually decides the choice is what happens to the surface over years of untreated irrigation water: corrosion, cavitation erosion, and then whether the shape can be cast cheaply.

07 · SELECTING THE BLADE MATERIAL

Criteria, weights and the winner

The selection is a weighted decision matrix. Each candidate is scored from 1 to 5 against five criteria: corrosion resistance, cavitation erosion resistance, castability into thin curved blades, cost, and stiffness per unit mass (the plate index of the Ashby chart, scaled to the best candidate). The weights say what matters, and you can change them. The scores are engineering judgement for untreated irrigation water, not measurements, so the ranking is a reasoned argument rather than a proof.

With the starting weights the winner is at , followed by . The bronzes and stainless steels lead because corrosion and cavitation carry more than half the weight. The cast irons are cheap and easy to cast but corrode and erode. Aluminium and glass-filled nylon are light, and the Ashby chart even prefers them, but they score 1 on cavitation and are screened out: untick the box to see them climb the table, and note that lightness alone then buys them a place a pump could not safely give them. Push cost up and the irons come back, which is exactly the compromise a real designer makes for the casing. The casing is cast iron, because it is a stiff, cheap, damping pressure shell that sees the water only on its inside. The impeller takes the highest-ranked bronze or stainless, because the blades are where cavitation and corrosion strike. The shaft is stainless steel where it passes the seal, and the seal faces are carbon against silicon carbide.
