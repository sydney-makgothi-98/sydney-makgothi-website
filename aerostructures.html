01 — WING ANALYSIS

Wing structural analysis: from root to tip

A wing is a cantilever beam that has to carry the whole aircraft. This project was about understanding exactly how that load travels from the tip to the root — first with an analytical model in MATLAB, then validated with a full finite element model.

Estimating bending stress in MATLAB

I started by building a MATLAB model of the wing as a tapered cantilever beam. Using the spanwise lift distribution, I integrated from the tip inwards to get the shear force and bending moment at every station along the span, then combined that with the section properties at each station to estimate the bending stress from root to tip.

$$\sigma_b(y) = \frac{M(y)\,c(y)}{I(y)}$$

Because the wing tapers, both the bending moment \(M(y)\) and the second moment of area \(I(y)\) change along the span, so the peak stress isn't simply "wherever the moment is largest". Scripting it in MATLAB made it quick to sweep through stations, plot the distribution and see where the structure was working hardest.

Wing cross-section

To get the section properties at each spanwise station, I idealised the wing box as a thin-walled cross-section made up of skin panels, spar webs and discrete reinforcing elements. Two types of reinforcement carry most of the bending load:

Stiffeners — L-shaped elementsStringers attached along the skin between the spars. They stabilise the skin panels against buckling and add bending stiffness.
Spar caps — T-shaped elementsFlanges at the top and bottom of each spar web. They carry the bulk of the bending load as tension and compression.

Finite element analysis: Nastran & Patran

To check the hand model, I carried out a Finite Element Analysis (FEA) of the full wing. MSC Patran was used as the pre- and post-processing interface — building the mesh, assigning material and section properties, and applying the loads and root boundary conditions — while MSC Nastran was used as the solver.

Modelling the complete wing from root to tip, the FEA captures what a beam model can't: how the skins and spars share load, and the coupling between bending and twist. Comparing the two approaches gave me confidence in both.

Results

The three key outputs of the analysis were the spanwise deflection, bending and twist of the wing.

02 — FUSELAGE CROSS-SECTION

Pressurised fuselage frame of an experimental jet fighter

The second project looked at a single fuselage frame of an experimental jet fighter, built up from identical bolted segments, and asked a simple question: is this design feasible once the fuselage is pressurised — even when the final joint doesn't line up?

The brief

SYNOPSIS OF THE INVESTIGATION

To reduce manufacturing costs, the fuselage frames of an experimental aircraft are each built up from 2n+1 identical segments. Each segment is built of two circular sections. Rout, n and Rcorner define the outer surface of each segment, and the inner circular surface is defined by the radial dimensions of the section at the midpoint, h1, and at the interface between adjacent segments, h2. The geometry of one segment of the frame is shaded in the figure below. Adjacent segments are illustrated without shading.

The frame is manufactured from aluminium (E = 73 GPa, υ = 0.3). Rcorner is 1000 mm. The frame has a constant thickness of 10 mm. The frame carries the internal pressure of the fuselage which is transferred to it by the skin on the outer surface. The frame spacing is 300 mm. Assume that the interior of the fuselage is pressurized to 0.5 bar.

Dimensions Rout, h1, h2 and the integer value of n are as indicated in the accompanying table.

Each frame is built up by bolting together the requisite number of segments. It can be assumed that there is no change in overall frame stiffness at the bolted joints. When the final bolts are to be installed at the top of the frame, it is found that the frame segments have not been perfectly aligned and that the two ends of the frame do not align perfectly. The joint is offset in both x and y directions and is also rotated. The misalignment of the neutral axis of the right side of the joint from that of the left is illustrated below.

The goal was to determine whether the design was feasible under internal pressurisation. To answer that, I looked at:

  • The stress variation radially along the frame
  • The rotation along the frame
  • The displacements in the x, y and radial directions

Each of these was evaluated for two load cases: the frame with only the joint misalignment forced closed (no pressure), and the same frame with internal pressure applied.

How the code was developed

01 Complementary energy methods

A closed frame is statically indeterminate, so equilibrium alone can't give the internal loads. I cut the frame at the joint and treated the internal bending moment, axial force and shear force there — \(M_a\), \(N_a\) and \(V_a\) — as the redundant unknowns. The total complementary energy of the frame is:

$$U^{*}=\oint \left(\frac{M^{2}}{2EI}+\frac{N^{2}}{2EA}+\frac{\kappa V^{2}}{2GA}\right)ds$$
  • \(M, N, V\): the total internal bending moment, axial force and shear force at any section \(s\), written as functions of the external loads and the redundant forces \((M_a, N_a, V_a)\).
  • \(EI\): bending stiffness (flexural rigidity).
  • \(EA\): axial stiffness.
  • \(GA/\kappa\): shear stiffness, where \(\kappa\) (or \(\alpha\)) is the shear correction factor depending on the cross-sectional shape.

Differentiating with respect to each redundant and setting it equal to the known misalignment at the joint gives one compatibility equation per unknown:

$$\frac{\partial U^{*}}{\partial M_a} = \theta_0, \qquad \frac{\partial U^{*}}{\partial N_a} = \delta_{x,0}, \qquad \frac{\partial U^{*}}{\partial V_a} = \delta_{y,0}$$

Collecting these gives an \(m \times m\) flexibility system (here \(m = 3\)), which the code assembles and solves for the internal loads:

$$\begin{bmatrix} f_{MM} & f_{MN} & f_{MV} \\ f_{NM} & f_{NN} & f_{NV} \\ f_{VM} & f_{VN} & f_{VV} \end{bmatrix} \begin{Bmatrix} M_a \\ N_a \\ V_a \end{Bmatrix} = \begin{Bmatrix} \theta_0 \\ \delta_{x,0} \\ \delta_{y,0} \end{Bmatrix} - \begin{Bmatrix} D_M \\ D_N \\ D_V \end{Bmatrix}$$

Each flexibility coefficient \(f_{ij}\) is itself an integral around the frame, and \(D_i\) is the contribution from the pressure load — which is where numerical integration comes in.

02 Numerical integration — the trapezoidal rule

Because the frame is made of circular arcs with a varying section depth between \(h_1\) and \(h_2\), the integrands don't have convenient closed-form solutions. Instead, the code discretises the frame into many small arc-length steps and integrates numerically using the trapezoidal rule:

$$\int_{a}^{b} f(s)\,ds \;\approx\; \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{\Delta s}{2}\left[f(s_{k-1}) + f(s_{k})\right]$$

03 Object-oriented programming

Rather than one long script, I structured the code using Object-Oriented Programming. Each part of the problem lives in its own class with a clear responsibility, so the same building blocks can be reused for a different frame geometry, material or load case without rewriting the solver.

Geometry

Segment dimensions (Rout, Rcorner, h1, h2, n) and section properties along the arc.

Material

Elastic properties (E, υ, G) kept separate so alloys can be swapped easily.

Loads

Internal pressure and joint misalignment as independent, combinable load cases.

Solver

Assembles the flexibility matrix, integrates numerically and solves for Ma, Na, Va.

This made the code modular, easier to test piece by piece, and reusable for future frame and ring problems.
