MANOEUVRE 01

The spiral climb

A spiral climb is a steady climb and a level turn flown at the same time. The aircraft banks to turn at a constant rate while its excess power lifts it at a constant rate of climb, so its path traces a helix. It's how aircraft gain height over a fixed point, for example to climb out of a valley or stay over an airfield.

The turn and the climb can be treated separately. The bank angle \(\phi\) tilts the lift vector, and its horizontal part pulls the aircraft round the circle. For a given true airspeed \(V\), that sets the turn rate, the radius and the load factor:

$$\omega = \frac{g\tan\phi}{V}, \qquad R = \frac{V^2}{g\tan\phi}, \qquad n = \frac{1}{\cos\phi}$$

The climb comes from excess power. The rate of climb fixes the flight-path angle, and dividing the height to gain by it gives the time in the spiral, and from the turn rate, the number of full turns:

$$\gamma = \sin^{-1}\!\left(\frac{RC}{V}\right), \qquad t = \frac{\Delta h}{RC}, \qquad N = \frac{\omega\, t}{2\pi}$$
  • \(RC\): rate of climb, set by the excess power \((T - D)\,V / W\).
  • \(\Delta h\): height gained, here from the ground to 11,000 ft.
  • Banking raises the load factor, which raises induced drag and eats into the excess power. A steeper bank therefore turns tighter but climbs more slowly for the same engine.

ANALYSIS 02

Where the analysis was done

The aerodynamic performance and stability of the Carbon Cub were analysed at one flight condition: 10,000 ft at Mach 0.35. Everything that follows, the trim curves, the stability derivatives and the dynamic modes, belongs to that point in the envelope.

ALTITUDE10,000 ft3,048 m, ISA standard day
SPEED OF SOUND328.4 m/sT = 268.3 K
AIR DENSITY0.905 kg/m³74 % of sea level
FLIGHT MACH NUMBER0.35V ≈ 115 m/s, about 223 kt

The speed of sound depends only on the air temperature, and the flight speed follows from the Mach number:

$$a = \sqrt{\gamma R T} = \sqrt{1.4 \times 287.05 \times 268.3} = 328.4\ \text{m/s}$$ $$V = M\,a = 0.35 \times 328.4 \approx 115\ \text{m/s}$$

At this speed the flow is slow enough for incompressible flow theory to be applied. How much does the air's density actually change? Where the airflow is brought to rest, at a stagnation point, the isentropic relation gives the largest change a surface will see:

$$\frac{\rho_0}{\rho_\infty} = \left(1 + \frac{\gamma - 1}{2}M^2\right)^{\frac{1}{\gamma - 1}} \approx 1 + \frac{M^2}{2} = 1 + \frac{0.35^2}{2} \approx 1.06$$

About 6 % at the very worst point. The usual rule of thumb is that density changes below roughly 5 % (M < 0.3) can be ignored. M = 0.35 sits just above that line, so the incompressible results are good for design work, and if a correction is wanted the Prandtl–Glauert factor \(1/\sqrt{1-M^2} = 1.068\) scales the lift-curve slope by under 7 %. Above M ≈ 0.4 to 0.5 that stops being true, as the next panel shows.

THE EFFECT ON PRESSUREPrandtl–Glauert correction

For incompressible flow the pressure coefficient on a cylinder follows from Bernoulli. Compressibility steepens it by the Prandtl–Glauert factor (more accurately, the Kármán–Tsien rule used in the readouts above):

$$C_p = 1 - \left(\frac{V}{U_\infty}\right)^2 = 1 - 4\sin^2\theta, \qquad\qquad C_p \approx \frac{C_{p,0}}{\sqrt{1 - M_\infty^2}}$$

WHY IT HELPSWhy the incompressible assumption made the analysis easier

  1. Density is a constant, not a variable. Continuity collapses to \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{V} = 0\). The momentum equations no longer depend on the energy equation, so there is no temperature, no equation of state and no speed of sound to carry around.
  2. The flow becomes linear. The velocity potential obeys Laplace's equation \(\nabla^2\phi = 0\). Solutions can simply be added together, which is the whole idea behind panel and vortex-lattice methods: build the wing from horseshoe vortices and superpose them.
  3. Pressure comes straight from Bernoulli. \(p + \tfrac12\rho V^2 = \text{const}\), so \(C_p = 1 - (V/U_\infty)^2\) with no Mach number in it.
  4. The stability derivatives stop depending on speed. The Mach-dependent terms \(C_{D_u}\), \(C_{L_u}\) and \(C_{m_u}\) drop to zero, which removes terms from the state matrix and leaves \(C_{L_\alpha}\), \(C_{m_\alpha}\) and the rest as constants.
  5. It is cheap. A vortex-lattice solution is one linear system of equations. It runs in seconds, so the CG, the tail and the control surfaces can be swept freely.
ANALYSIS 04

Static stability: Cm and trim curves

Static stability asks one question: if the nose is disturbed, does the aircraft push it back? The answer is the slope of the pitching-moment curve, and it is controlled almost entirely by where the centre of gravity sits relative to the neutral point.

DERIVATION 1Pitching moment about the CG

Take moments about the CG for the wing and body, whose lift acts at the aerodynamic centre \(h_0\), and for the tail a distance \(\ell_T\) behind it, where \(\bar V_T = S_T\ell_T/(S\bar c)\) is the tail volume coefficient:

$$C_m = C_{m_0w} + C_{L_w}(h - h_0) - \bar V_T\, C_{L_T}$$

The tail sees the angle of attack reduced by the downwash \(\varepsilon = \varepsilon_0 + \frac{d\varepsilon}{d\alpha}\alpha\) and has its lift changed by the elevator:

$$C_{L_T} = a_1\left(\alpha - \varepsilon + \eta_T\right) + a_2\,\eta$$

Substituting, and collecting everything that multiplies lift, gives a compact form. The CG position \(h\) and a single point \(h_n\), the neutral point, are all that matter:

$$C_m = C_{m_0} + C_L\,(h - h_n) + C_{m_\eta}\,\eta, \qquad h_n = h_0 + \bar V_T\,\frac{a_1}{a}\left(1 - \frac{d\varepsilon}{d\alpha}\right), \qquad C_{m_\eta} = -\bar V_T\,a_2$$

Define the static margin \(K_n = h_n - h\). The slope of the \(C_m\)–\(\alpha\) curve is then:

$$C_{m_\alpha} = \frac{\partial C_m}{\partial\alpha} = a\,(h - h_n) = -\,a\,K_n$$

A CG ahead of the neutral point (\(K_n > 0\)) gives a negative slope. A nose-up disturbance makes a nose-down moment, so the aircraft is statically stable. A CG behind it gives a positive slope, and any disturbance grows.

DERIVATION 2Trim: how much elevator at each speed

In steady flight the moment is zero and lift equals weight. Setting \(C_m = 0\) and \(C_L = 2W/\rho V^2 S\) gives the elevator needed to trim at any speed:

$$\eta_{trim}(V) = -\frac{C_{m_0} + C_L(h - h_n)}{C_{m_\eta}} = \frac{K_n\,C_L - C_{m_0}}{C_{m_\eta}}, \qquad C_L = \frac{2W}{\rho V^2 S}$$

The sign of the slope of that curve is the stick-fixed stability test. Differentiating with respect to speed:

$$\frac{d\eta_{trim}}{dV} = -\,\frac{4\,K_n\,W}{\rho\,S\,C_{m_\eta}\,V^3} \;>\; 0 \quad\text{when } K_n > 0 \text{ and } C_{m_\eta} < 0$$

A stable aircraft needs progressively less up-elevator as it speeds up, so holding a faster speed takes a push forward on the stick. Move the CG aft until the slope flattens and then reverses and the aircraft has lost that feel entirely.

ANALYSIS 05

Stability derivatives and the equations of motion

Static stability only tells us the direction of the restoring moment. To find out what the aircraft does in time after a disturbance we need the stability derivatives and a set of equations to put them in.

WHERE THE DERIVATIVES CAME FROMThree independent sources

The derivatives were obtained three ways, so that each result could be checked against the other two:

METHOD 1

Tornado VLM

A MATLAB vortex-lattice code. The wing, tail and fin are panelled with horseshoe vortices and the induced velocities are solved as one linear system.

It returns \(C_{L_\alpha}\), \(C_{m_\alpha}\), the rate and control derivatives and the neutral point directly from the geometry.

METHOD 2

Athena Vortex Lattice

AVL by Mark Drela is a second vortex-lattice solver. It builds its own model of the aircraft and linearises it about the trim condition.

It returns the full set of stability-axis derivatives, so it checks Tornado and gives a second view of the modes.

METHOD 3

Analytical, after Cook

Closed-form estimates from geometry, following Flight Dynamics Principles. For example \(C_{m_\alpha} = -aK_n\), with tail volume setting the pitch damping.

It makes the physics visible and gives hand-checkable numbers to compare the lattice codes against.

All three are linear, inviscid methods, which is why the incompressible assumption from the last section sat so comfortably underneath them. Each gives a dimensionless coefficient. Cook's conversion to the dimensional derivatives used in the equations of motion, for incompressible flow with \(C_{D_u} = C_{L_u} = C_{m_u} = 0\), is:

$$X_u = -\frac{\rho V S}{m}C_D,\quad X_w = \frac{\rho V S}{2m}\left(C_L - C_{D_\alpha}\right)$$ $$Z_u = -\frac{\rho V S}{m}C_L,\quad Z_w = -\frac{\rho V S}{2m}\left(C_{L_\alpha} + C_D\right)$$ $$M_w = \frac{\rho V S\bar c}{2I_y}\,C_{m_\alpha},\quad M_q = \frac{\rho V S\bar c^{\,2}}{2I_y}\,C_{m_q}$$ $$Z_\eta = -\frac{\rho V^2 S}{2m}C_{L_\eta},\quad M_\eta = \frac{\rho V^2 S\bar c}{2I_y}\,C_{m_\eta}$$

STEP 1Equations of motion

Newton's second law for the aircraft in body axes, restricted to symmetric flight in the vertical plane (no sideslip, roll or yaw), with pitch angle \(\theta\) and pitch rate \(q\):

$$m\left(\dot u + q\,w + g\sin\theta\right) = X, \qquad m\left(\dot w - q\,u - g\cos\theta\right) = Z, \qquad I_y\,\dot q = M, \qquad \dot\theta = q$$

These are nonlinear: they contain the products \(qw\) and \(qu\), and the trigonometric terms in \(\theta\). \(X\), \(Z\) and \(M\) are the aerodynamic and thrust forces and the moment, which depend on \(u\), \(w\), \(q\) and the elevator \(\eta\).

STEP 2Linearisation with a Maclaurin series

Write each variable as the steady trim value plus a small perturbation, for example \(u = U_e + u\), \(w = 0 + w\). Each aerodynamic term is then expanded as a Taylor series about the trim point. Because the perturbations start from zero, this is the special case of the Taylor series known as the Maclaurin series, and we keep only the first-order terms:

$$X = X_e + \frac{\partial X}{\partial u}u + \frac{\partial X}{\partial w}w + \frac{\partial X}{\partial q}q + \frac{\partial X}{\partial \eta}\eta + \frac12\frac{\partial^2 X}{\partial u^2}u^2 + \cdots$$ $$X \approx X_e + X_u u + X_w w + X_q q + X_\eta\eta$$

The same trick turns the trigonometric terms into simple polynomials, and the products of two small perturbations are dropped as second order:

$$\sin\theta = \theta - \frac{\theta^3}{3!} + \frac{\theta^5}{5!} - \cdots \approx \theta, \qquad \cos\theta = 1 - \frac{\theta^2}{2!} + \cdots \approx 1, \qquad q\,w \approx 0, \qquad q\,u \approx q\,U_e$$

Putting the expansions back into the equations of motion and moving the trim terms to cancel (the trim values satisfy the equations on their own) leaves a linear set, written for level trim in stability axes:

$$\dot u = X_u u + X_w w - g\,\theta, \qquad \dot w = Z_u u + Z_w w + U_e\,q + Z_\eta\eta, \qquad \dot q = M_w w + M_q q + M_\eta\eta, \qquad \dot\theta = q$$

STEP 3State-space form

Collect the four variables into a state vector \(\mathbf{x} = [u,\ w,\ q,\ \theta]^T\) and the elevator into the input, and the linear equations become one matrix equation:

$$\dot{\mathbf{x}} = A\,\mathbf{x} + B\,\eta, \qquad A = \begin{bmatrix} X_u & X_w & 0 & -g \\ Z_u & Z_w & U_e & 0 \\ 0 & M_w & M_q & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ Z_\eta \\ M_\eta \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

STEP 4Laplace transform: from matrices to time behaviour

Take the Laplace transform of the state equation. Differentiation becomes multiplication by \(s\), and the initial disturbance \(\mathbf{x}(0)\) appears as a source term:

$$s\,\mathbf{X}(s) - \mathbf{x}(0) = A\,\mathbf{X}(s) + B\,\eta(s) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathbf{X}(s) = (sI - A)^{-1}\left[\mathbf{x}(0) + B\,\eta(s)\right]$$ $$\mathbf{X}(s) = \frac{\operatorname{adj}(sI - A)}{\Delta(s)}\left[\mathbf{x}(0) + B\,\eta(s)\right]$$

Everything about the dynamics is in the denominator, the characteristic polynomial \(\Delta(s) = \det(sI - A)\). Its roots are the eigenvalues of \(A\), the aircraft's poles. After a disturbance with the stick fixed (\(\eta = 0\)), splitting the response into partial fractions and transforming back gives the time behaviour, a sum of one term per pole:

$$\mathbf{x}(t) = \sum_i \mathbf{c}_i\,e^{\lambda_i t}, \qquad \lambda = -\zeta\omega_n \pm j\,\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2} \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; e^{-\zeta\omega_n t}\cos\!\left(\omega_d t + \varphi\right)$$

A pole in the left half-plane decays, one in the right half-plane grows, and its imaginary part sets the oscillation frequency. For this aircraft at the chosen CG and speed the polynomial and the elevator-to-pitch transfer function are:

ANALYSIS 06

Phugoid and short-period motion

The two pole pairs are two physically different motions. They are separated by a factor of more than a hundred in frequency, so they can be understood almost independently.

FAST · SECONDS

Short period

The aircraft pitches about its CG while the speed stays almost constant. The tail's restoring moment (\(M_w\)) fights the inertia, and pitch damping (\(M_q\)) bleeds the motion away. It sets how crisp the aircraft feels in pitch.

SLOW · MINUTES

Phugoid

The aircraft swaps height for speed along a long, gentle, wavy path at nearly constant angle of attack. It is a pendulum of kinetic and potential energy, damped only by drag. Lanchester's classic estimate gives \(\omega_p \approx g\sqrt2/V\).

ANALYSIS 07

Routh–Hurwitz stability criterion

Finding the poles by solving the polynomial works, but the Routh–Hurwitz criterion answers the stability question without solving it, directly from the coefficients. It was used to verify the result of the eigenvalue analysis and to see how close to the edge the design sits.

For a characteristic polynomial \(\Delta(s) = a_n s^n + a_{n-1}s^{n-1} + \dots + a_0\), build the Routh array. The first two rows are the coefficients, taken alternately. Every row below is computed from the two above it:

$$b_i = \frac{a_{n-1}\,a_{n-2i} - a_n\,a_{n-2i-1}}{a_{n-1}}$$

Each later row follows from the same rule applied to the two rows above it.

The number of sign changes in the first column equals the number of poles in the right half-plane. A stable aircraft has none. For the quartic \(A s^4 + B s^3 + C s^2 + D s + E\) of longitudinal flight dynamics, this reduces to Hurwitz's conditions:

$$A,\,B,\,C,\,D,\,E > 0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad R = B\,C\,D - A\,D^2 - B^2E > 0$$

\(R\) is called Routh's discriminant. When \(E\) goes negative the aircraft has a real, slow divergence, which is static instability, and when \(R\) goes negative the phugoid has become an oscillation that grows. Try the CG slider below. At \(K_n = 0\) the constant term \(E\) reaches zero in this model, the CG is at the neutral point, and a pole sits at the origin.

    ANALYSIS 08

    Flight control: balancing the modes with PID

    The bare airframe is stable here, but a pilot would not want to fly a phugoid that takes minutes to die away, or an airframe that needs constant correction in turbulence. A flight control system closes a loop around the aircraft: it measures the motion, compares it with what is wanted, and moves the elevator to cancel the difference.

    A PID controller does that with three terms acting on the pitch-attitude error \(e = \theta_{cmd} - \theta\). Each one balances a different part of the motion:

    $$\eta_{cmd} = -\left[K_p\,e + K_i\!\int e\,dt + K_d\,\dot e\right], \qquad G_c(s) = K_p + \frac{K_i}{s} + K_d\,s$$
    • P, proportional: acts like extra pitch stiffness. It pulls \(\theta\) back to the commanded value, and because the phugoid is an exchange of height and speed through the pitch angle, holding \(\theta\) breaks that exchange and the phugoid has no energy to oscillate with.
    • D, derivative: acts on the pitch rate \(q\) from a rate gyro and adds damping. It is the part that tames the short period, like adding extra \(C_{m_q}\) without changing the aircraft.
    • I, integral: keeps adding correction for as long as an error remains, so a steady disturbance such as a speed change or CG shift ends with zero steady-state error and a trimmed aircraft.

    In the block diagram, the elevator command passes through an actuator lag, then the aircraft. Pitch rate and pitch angle are measured and fed back, and a gust enters at the aircraft. The closed-loop characteristic equation is \(1 + G_c(s)\,G_{act}(s)\,G_{\theta/\eta}(s) = 0\), and its roots are the new poles. The animated signal flow follows the loop.
