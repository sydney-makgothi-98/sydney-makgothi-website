Senior Design Project · Electric Flight

The one percent problem

The Spirit of Innovation is the fastest all-electric aircraft in the world. The brief was to make it go one percent faster: a smaller wing, a bigger propeller, and a great deal of checking.

By Gopolang MakgothiFrom the senior design report, under the supervision of Mr Michael F Boer2025

The Rolls-Royce Spirit of Innovation is a modified Sharp Nemesis NXT racing airframe, driven by three YASA 750R electric motors through one three-blade propeller. It makes 400 kW (535 hp) from a 750 V battery of about 6,000 cells and 90 kWh, flies at a take-off mass of about 1,250 kg, and holds the speed record for electric aircraft. It was built under ACCEL, the “Accelerating the Electrification of Flight” programme, to show that electric propulsion can compete on performance and to promote zero-emission flight.

The senior design project began from a single observation: the record has stood since 2021, and a record that stands for years is a sign that nothing is catching it. The Fédération Aéronautique Internationale (FAI) says a new record must beat the old by 1 %. Taking the speed to beat as 155.28 m/s (559 km/h), that is 156.83 m/s, or 564.6 km/h. The question was how to get the Spirit of Innovation there, cheaply, without making it unstable, unsafe or illegal to certify.

What is the cheapest way to beat the record by one percent?The brief

The answer the report reached has four parts, and this article follows them in order. The wing was made smaller: the span reduced from 7.30 m to 7.11 m and the area from 6.33 m² to 6.14 m², with a 0.45 taper and a NACA 23015 section, built in carbon fibre. The tail was scaled to match, keeping the aircraft stable and its controls light enough to fly. The propeller was redesigned with a larger diameter and a slower tip, raising its efficiency from 80 % to 85 %. And everything was checked again: the structure, the centre of gravity, the weight, and the stability in all five of its modes. The report’s final top speed is 158 m/s.

Part oneThe competition, and the power balance

Before changing anything, the report asked what the Spirit of Innovation had already done right. Its only real competitor in the electric class is the Extra 330LE, a single-seat aerobatic monoplane with a 260 kW motor, which held the record from 2016 to 2021 with a published top speed of 343 km/h (95 m/s). The two aircraft were compared on powerplant, propeller, drag and wing, using thrust and drag curves, an activity-factor propeller model, a component drag build-up and Vortex Lattice models in Tornado for MATLAB.

The comparison put the Spirit ahead on four counts. Its powerplant makes 53.85 % more power than the Extra’s. Its propeller (an activity factor of 180 against 120, a radius of 0.683 m against 0.975 m) is 7.04 % more efficient at speeds of 90 m/s and above. It has retractable landing gear, where the Extra’s is fixed. And its sleeker wing produces about 8.33 % less induced drag. The drag build-up showed landing gear, fuselage and wing as the three largest contributors for both aircraft.

Two more checks closed the question of whether to modify or to start again. A plot of speed-to-weight ratio against year for the high-performance electric aircraft put the Spirit first, at 0.608 km/h per kg, with nothing close enough to modify. And the money pointed the same way: the Spirit cost about £6.9 million to develop, so a new aircraft would cost something similar, while the computational fluid dynamics for a modification costs between about $2,000 and $20,000, and certifying a changed aircraft has been quoted at up to $150,000. A modification was the only sensible route.

The power balance

In level flight at top speed the thrust power the propeller delivers equals the power the drag absorbs, and the top speed is where the two curves cross. The drag is the parasite drag of the whole aircraft plus the induced drag of the wing, which depends on the weight W and the wing.

$$\eta P_{shaft}=D\,V,\qquad D=\tfrac12\rho V^{2}S\,C_{D0}+\frac{2\,W^{2}}{\rho V^{2}S\,\pi\,e\,AR}$$

The report took the wing efficiency as 0.75 (from Cook), the parasite drag coefficient as CD0 = 0.01832 from a component build-up, a mass of 1248.6 kg and 535 hp, and an unmodified wing of 6.33 m² and 7.30 m span, an aspect ratio of about 8.4. The altitude is quoted as 3,000 m and, in the drag table, as 9,048 ft, which is 2,758 m; the model here uses the air density at 3,000 m, 0.909 kg/m³. The drivetrain efficiency is not published, so it is taken as the single number that makes the baseline reproduce the report’s 158.41 m/s. Every comparison below is then a ratio against the same baseline.

The method of net outcomes

To find which parameters could give 1 %, the specific excess power equation was broken down as far as it would go, a technique the report calls the method of net outcomes: each term splits into the things that can be changed, until what is left is a list of knobs. Thrust per unit mass depends on the number of cells and on the propeller; drag per unit mass on the parasite drag (wing area, the scrubbing drag of the propeller) and on the induced drag (span, area, aerofoil).

An energy–manoeuvrability chart, drawn for the aircraft before and after, then showed how far the zero line of specific excess power had to move: a 3.09 % increase in thrust or a 3.09 % reduction in drag was enough to take the top speed from 155 m/s to 156.55 m/s. A sensitivity analysis turned that into three routes.

The three user requirements that follow from this are the report’s starting point: a top speed of 564.6 km/h or more over 3 km; either 3.17 % more horsepower or 3.09 % less wing area; and, as constraints, an all-electric powerplant, a mass no greater than 1.75 tonnes, a centre of gravity kept 18 % ahead of the aerodynamic centre, flight no higher or lower than 3,000 m, and FAI and FAR 23 regulations. The limit load factor had to be at least 3.61 g and the ultimate 4.30 g.

Part twoMore power, or less wing

Routes that were rejected

More cells. The powertrain is 18 modules making 535.5 hp and weighing about 700 kg, so one more module is worth 29.6 hp (5.53 %) and costs 38.9 kg (3.07 % of the aircraft). That would move the centre of gravity back from 18 % to about 15 % ahead of the aerodynamic centre, making the aircraft less nose-heavy and less stable, and the schematics show no room for the extra cells inside the fuselage. Lengthening the fuselage would mean redesigning and recertifying the airframe. The route was dropped.

A higher aspect ratio. To cut the area by 3.09 % through aspect ratio, the aspect ratio would have to rise by about 4 %, which by NASA’s FLOPS wing-weight method takes the aircraft from 1248.6 kg to about 1286.6 kg and raises the parasite drag coefficient from 0.0183 to 0.019. The specific excess power curves showed the energy margin falling, not rising. The route was dropped.

The route that worked: a shorter span

By elimination, the wing area had to come down through the span. Reducing it from 7.30 m to 7.11 m takes the area from 6.33 m² to 6.14 m² (3.0 %), lowers the aspect ratio from 8.44 to 8.22, and makes the wing lighter. With the span and area fixed, the planform was a matter of taper.

The aerofoil came first. A thicker section raises the form factor and so the parasite drag, which defeats the purpose, so the 16 % thickness of the existing wing was set as a limit. Lift and pitching-moment data for the NACA four- and five-digit sections thinner than that were extracted in XFLR5 (the six-series is not supported there). The NACA 23015 won on two counts: the lowest stall speed, and a pitching moment near zero at low angles of attack, like a symmetric section.

Why not a fully elliptical wing, then? No published figure for its cost could be found, so I estimated it from complexity: spars are simplest when straight, harder when tapered and hardest when elliptical, and the development times of two wartime fighters (about five years for the elliptical-winged Spitfire, three for the Westland F.7/30) were used to scale the labour cost. These are high-level estimates, ignoring the materials and labour costs of the 1930s, but they showed the elliptical planform costing far more than a tapered one that gives a very similar lift distribution.

The model reproduces the report’s own finding for the wing: a gain of about 0.9 % from the smaller area, a little less than the 1 % needed. The report’s 156.55 m/s is 1 % above a baseline of 155.0 m/s; against the 155.28 m/s record used for the target (156.83 m/s) the wing on its own falls just short, which is why the propeller matters (Part six).

Where the wing goes

The last wing decision was its height on the fuselage. A mid or high wing improves lateral stability in a turn, but needs external landing gear, and the Extra 330LE’s fixed gear is one of the reasons it is slower. A low wing lets the gear retract into the wing. A flight-dynamics study (Part four) then checked that a low wing is stable enough in manoeuvres.

Part threeInside the new wing

A smaller wing is a more heavily loaded wing, so choosing the planform is only half of the job; the other half is making it strong enough. The structure was designed in sequence, a component at a time, against the lift distribution given by Schrenk’s approximation and checked with computational fluid dynamics on a three-dimensional model of the wing in level flight, because Tornado and XFLR5 both lose accuracy past Mach 0.35. The wing was compared with the lift of a fully elliptical wing from Prandtl’s lifting-line theory to see how efficient it is. The structure is conventional: ribs hold the shape, spars carry the bending, stringers keep the skin from buckling, and the skin carries the twist and the pressure.

The skin

Three skin materials were compared on stiffness for the weight and the smoothness of the surface, since a rough skin costs drag. The merit used was Young’s modulus over density over average surface roughness, with data from the Granta database. Carbon-fibre reinforced polymer won by a wide margin.

Skin thickness was set by buckling. Treating one panel as a flat plate held on all four edges, a panel with an aspect ratio of one has the highest buckling coefficient, about 14. A panel of 100 mm by 100 mm (a Boeing 747’s are about 200 mm, and the Spirit is far smaller) in 1 mm carbon fibre buckles at about 52 MPa, and at the root, the most heavily loaded section, that is 2.6 times the shear stress acting on it. Thicker skin would be safer and heavier; 1 mm was the compromise.

Plate performance and buckling

A skin panel between two ribs and two stringers is a flat plate, and what limits it is not its strength but its stability: a thin plate loaded in its own plane, in compression or shear, suddenly bows out of plane at a load well below the one that would crush or tear it. How a plate behaves in bending is set by its flexural rigidity D, and the load at which it buckles by D, the plate width b and a buckling coefficient k:

$$D=\frac{E\,t^{3}}{12\,(1-\nu^{2})},\qquad N_{cr}=k\,\frac{\pi^{2}D}{b^{2}},\qquad \sigma_{cr}=\frac{N_{cr}}{t}=k\,\frac{\pi^{2}E}{12\,(1-\nu^{2})}\left(\frac{t}{b}\right)^{2}$$

Here E is the modulus of elasticity of the skin, ν its Poisson ratio, t the thickness, and b the loaded-edge width of the panel of length a. For shear the same expression is used with a shear buckling coefficient ks in place of k, and the result is compared with the shear stress found from the shear flow. The strong message in the equation is the square of t/b: halving the panel width is as good as doubling the thickness, which is why a skin is stiffened with stringers and ribs instead of being made thick.

The coefficient k depends on the aspect ratio a/b of the panel and on how its edges are held, and Fig. 16(a) gives it: a plate clamped on all its edges has the highest values, and a/b = 1 is where a square panel does best, with k about 14, which is the value used. As a check on the 52.34 MPa the report finds for the 1 mm, 100 mm square panel: with k = 14 and ν = 0.3 it works back to a modulus of about 41 GPa. That is where a laminate with a mix of 0° and ±45° plies sits on the carpet plot, and well below the 200 GPa of the fibre alone, because buckling depends on the stiffness of the laminate, not of the fibre.

Stiffeners: sections, relations and uses

A stringer takes load along the span and divides the skin into smaller panels, which raises the buckling stress of each. The stringer itself can fail in a way a plate cannot: its own flanges can buckle locally and fold, which is called crippling. The crippling stress of a section is found from an empirical relation in which the cross-section enters through the number of flanges and cuts, g, the cross-sectional area A, the thickness t, the modulus E and the compressive yield stress σcy:

$$\frac{\sigma_{cc}}{\sigma_{cy}}=C\left[\frac{g\,t^{2}}{A}\sqrt{\frac{E}{\sigma_{cy}}}\right]^{m}$$

C and m are empirical constants that depend on the material and the section, g counts the flanges, and a section with a large g and a small area for its thickness is made of many thin, well-supported flanges. The relation says that the more the material is spread into well-supported flanges, the nearer the section can get to its full yield stress before it cripples. The report chose between them as a compromise. Tubes have the highest crippling strength, but they are poor in torsion, and a wing that twists under load with poor torsional stiffness invites flutter, so I gave up a little crippling strength for a section that behaves well in torsion: an L-shaped stringer of 100 mm².

Spars, stringers and ribs

To save weight the wing has two spars, a front and a rear, with 2 mm webs. The front spar stands 173 mm high at the root and 99.75 mm at the tip (the same 0.45 taper as the wing); the rear spar is 77.85 mm throughout. For the caps, I-channel and C-channel sections were compared with the same web and height. The I-channel has the larger second moment of area (46.4 against 38.3 million mm⁴) and so the lower bending stress, and was chosen.

The stringers stop the skin buckling between the ribs. Crippling strength was worked out for a range of sections in 20 mm thick carbon fibre, taking the compressive strength as about 30 % of the tensile (689 MPa). As explained above, the choice was an L-shaped stringer of 100 mm². The ribs are carbon fibre with six relief holes, as seen in light sporting aircraft. Shear flow was worked out round the root rib, and deflection, twist and stress were checked in a finite-element model in SimScale.

Making it

Two methods suit a one-off aircraft because their tooling is cheap: hand lay-up, in which dry fabric is placed on a mould and wetted with resin by hand, and vacuum bagging, in which the lay-up is sealed in a bag and the air pumped out, compacting it and removing voids. Published tests give vacuum-bagged specimens about 28.57 % more strength than hand lay-up, so vacuum bagging was recommended for the skin. The recommended route is: choose the fibre (Toray T700SC, whose modulus meets the 200 GPa target), stack the plies at the angles a carpet plot gives, heat-treat the stretched fibre (about 1,800 °C is recommended for the modulus), vacuum-bag and cure, trim, inspect by ultrasound and coat for smoothness.

Part fourIs it stable?

A smaller wing and a smaller tail change how the aircraft responds when it is disturbed, so the report repeated the flight-dynamics analysis for the modified aircraft. The mass moments of inertia were estimated by following Roskam’s method (interpolating the known moments of inertia of other aircraft against mass): Ixx = 1,521.9, Izz = 3,176.2 and Ixz = 158.8 kg·m². The result is that the modified aircraft is stable in both longitudinal modes, in the Dutch roll and in roll, and unstable in the spiral. A spiral divergence is common and mild: the time constant of the spiral mode is 1 / 0.011 = 91 s, and the disturbance doubles in about 63 s, which leaves the pilot ample time to correct it before it develops. The report concluded that a low wing, with its stability and its low drag, is the right configuration. The aircraft was also run through elevator step inputs to confirm that the elevator, as sized in Part five, damps out a disturbance quickly.

Part fiveThe tail and the controls

With the wing smaller, the tail had to be resized so that the aircraft kept its pitch and yaw stability. The rule was to hold the aerodynamic tail length (3.754 m) and the tail volume coefficients constant, which scales the tail area down with the wing area. The spans were kept and the root chords reduced, since the root chord reduces area far more than the taper does and a smaller tip chord would cost the tail its torsional stiffness.

Both tails use a NACA 0009, the symmetric section with the highest lift-curve slope of those tried, which gives the elevator and rudder the most lift per degree. For the elevator, three balance designs (overhanging, horn, internal sealed) were compared by hinge moment, and the horn balance needed the least force at speed. A horn-balanced elevator was then compared with an all-moving stabiliser, taking 15° of deflection (a quarter is lost in flight to aerodynamic and aeroelastic effects) and a pilot who can pull the stick back 25 cm; the elevator needed less stick force, and the all-moving stabiliser was ruled out.

The elevator was then sized against the FAR 23 stick-force limits: no more than 3 kg of force for sustained flight and 12 to 15 kg for short periods, such as a high-g turn. Trim curves from 1 to 4 g, at 156.55 m/s and 9,048 ft, showed that the worst case needs −2.89° of elevator at 4 g. Of four elevator sizes tried, the smallest (0.268 m²) gave the lowest stick force, 12.14 kg for that manoeuvre, and trimming it to about 0.25 m² brought the figure to 11.16 kg, inside the limit.

Part sixMore thrust: the propeller

The report notes that the smaller wing already meets its target on paper, so the thrust work was a buffer in case the geometric changes fell short. There were three ways to add thrust. A more powerful motor was ruled out: the YASA is already the most powerful all-electric aircraft motor, and the FAI requires an electric one. More cells was ruled out for the reasons in Part two. That left the propeller.

The propeller was first put at 80 % efficient, from the activity-factor model (an activity factor of 180). Raising it to 85 % gives 6.25 % more thrust from the same motor, and 85 % is about the best realistic figure, since most propellers are 80 to 85 % efficient. Blade Element Momentum Theory was used to find how, using a first-order approximation from Raymer that splits the change in shaft power into an induced part (about 30 %) and a profile part (about 70 %). The iteration gave the targets:

A larger disc moves more air more slowly, which is more efficient; the chord is increased so that the thrust coefficient, which depends on the solidity, stays acceptable as the tip slows. The new propeller still clears the ground, so no new landing gear was designed. With it, the report’s thrust and drag curves give a top speed of about 158 m/s. The cost is a slight reduction in the turn rate.

Part sevenDoes it still comply?

With the design complete, the report checked it against the requirements. The V–n diagram for the modified aircraft, drawn to FAR 23 with gust speeds of 15.28 and 7.62 m/s, still satisfies the limit and ultimate load factors. The centre-of-gravity envelope, drawn for masses between 490 kg and 1,750 kg, is unaffected, because the mass fell slightly and no cells were added, so the centre of gravity does not move. Part eightA critical evaluation

Much as I would like to say that the aircraft is 2 % faster, it is quite likely that it is only about 1 % faster, and barely at that. I did not analyse the masses of the components in detail, I left parts such as the air intakes out of the parasite-drag sum, my thrust models are not exact, and most of my inputs and outputs are estimates, including those from NASA’s FLOPS. It is therefore hard for me to say, with full confidence, that reducing the span and area to 7.11 m and 6.14 m² achieved a 1 % increase in speed.

Structurally, I left a good deal out. I did not check whether the wing’s natural frequency matches the fuselage’s after the modification (it is unlikely to, but one can never be too certain), I did not analyse torsional stiffness, and I did not compute control reversal, so I cannot say that the aircraft is safe to fly from an aeroelastic point of view. Aerodynamically, I did not investigate the effect of twist, geometric or aerodynamic, which means there is bias in my wing sizing and selection, and I could have constrained the tailplane more tightly to get a more objective design. All in all, I consider the design a success, but far more work can be done to improve the Spirit of Innovation.

What comes next

The recommended work is: aileron sizing, which the report demonstrates in MATLAB as a worked example (an aileron of about 1.68 m span with chords of 0.177 m and 0.080 m, starting 1.90 m from the root, meets the FAR 23.157 requirement to roll through 60° in 4 s); a vibration analysis of the wing, to get mode shapes and behaviour in hard manoeuvres, which is the subject of the aeroelasticity page; better mass estimates, by averaging methods such as Roskam’s with FLOPS; CFD over the entire aircraft, to include boundary-layer effects; and an investigation of twist.

A note on the numbers

  • How much faster. The report’s summary calls the result a 2 % gain. A final speed of 158 m/s is 1.75 % over the 155.28 m/s record, and 0.9 % over the 156.55 m/s that the wing change alone reached. The page uses 1.75 %.
  • The 1 % line. 156.55 m/s is 1 % above 155.0 m/s. Against the record of 155.28 m/s the 1 % line is 156.83 m/s, which the wing alone does not quite reach and the propeller does.
  • The spiral. The time constant of a mode with root 0.011 is 1 / 0.011 = 91 s (the report’s text says 83.33 s, which is 1 / 0.012); the doubling time is 63 s.
  • The aerofoil. The wing requirement in the report names a NACA 23012; every other table and the text use the NACA 23015, which the page follows.
  • The speed to beat. The introduction quotes 626.4 km/h for the record; the analysis uses 155.28 m/s (559 km/h), which the page follows.
  • Wing mass. 73.69 kg is the FLOPS estimate for the wing concept; the weight table uses 163.26 kg for the wing. They are different estimates in the report, and the page quotes each where the report does.
  • The model. The curves in this article are a simplified power balance, calibrated to the report’s own baseline. For the wing alone it agrees with the report. For the propeller the report’s 158 m/s is quoted and not modelled, since the simple model would give a larger gain than the report’s thrust curves do.
