The 1 % problem

Make the fastest electric aircraft in the world 1 % faster. Change the wing, run the 3 km course and see what a percent is worth.

ready

01 · THE CHALLENGE

The fastest electric aeroplane, and a 1 % target

The Rolls-Royce Spirit of Innovation is the fastest all-electric aircraft in the world. It is a modified Sharp Nemesis NXT racing airframe with three YASA 750R electric motors, a 400 kW power output, and about 6,000 cells in a 750 V battery of 216 kg storing 90 kWh, flying at a take-off mass of about 1,250 kg. The report this page comes from takes its 3 km record speed as 155.28 m/s (559 km/h) and asks one question: what is the cheapest way to beat it by 1 %, which is 156.83 m/s or 564.6 km/h, so that the next record is held by this design?

The question has a nice shape. A 1 % target is small enough that a modification of the existing aircraft should beat a new design on cost, but not so small that it is free. The honest first step is a model that says which variables move the top speed and by how much, and that is what the explorer in the stage above does. Press Run the record to see what the answer looks like: two aircraft, one 3 km course.

02 · THE MODEL

Power available against power required

In level flight at top speed the thrust power the propeller delivers equals the power the drag absorbs, and the top speed is where the two curves cross. The drag is the sum of the parasite drag of the whole aircraft and the induced drag of the wing, which depends on the weight W and the wing:

$$\eta P_{shaft}=D\,V,\qquad D=\tfrac12\rho V^{2}S\,C_{D0}+\frac{2\,W^{2}}{\rho V^{2}S\,\pi\,e\,AR}$$

The aircraft is flown at about 3,000 m (9,048 ft) in ISA conditions, so the air density is 0.909 kg/m³. The report estimated the parasite drag coefficient by a component drag build-up, CD0 = 0.01832, took the wing efficiency as 0.75 and the aspect ratio as 8.2 (span 7.3 m, area 6.5 m²). Checked against the published speeds, the build-up predicts 158.4 m/s for the Spirit of Innovation (2.1 % high) and 91.1 m/s for the competing Extra 330LE (4.2 % low), which is close enough to use as a baseline for a 1 % question, because every comparison below is a ratio against that same baseline.

The overall drivetrain efficiency is not published, so the explorer takes it as the single number that makes the baseline reproduce the report's 158.4 m/s (about 0.58). It then gives the same sensitivities the report found:

  • Drag. At top speed the drag is mostly parasite, so power required rises roughly with the cube of speed and a 1 % speed gain needs about 3 % less drag or 3 % more thrust power. The report's own figure is 3.81 %.
  • Wing efficiency. Raising the Oswald efficiency from 0.75 to 0.80 by making the lift distribution more elliptical changes the top speed by less than 0.2 %. At this speed the wing is lightly loaded, so induced drag is a small part of the total.
  • Wing area. Parasite drag is proportional to the wing area, so a smaller wing is faster: a 3 % reduction in area is worth about 1 % in speed. The wing area is the most direct lever.
03 · THE ROUTES

More power, or less wing

The report works in power per unit wing area, the quantity that sets how fast a given wing can be pushed. The unmodified aircraft has 82.4 hp/m² and a 1 % faster one needs 84.8, an increase of 2.96 %. Route 1: add power

The motors are built from 18 battery and motor modules which together give 535.35 hp and weigh about 700 kg, so each module is worth 29.6 hp and costs 38.9 kg. One extra module raises the power per unit area by 5.55 %, comfortably past the target. The cost is that it raises the mass by 3.07 %, lengthens the fuselage and moves the centre of gravity, which the report treats as a violation of its requirement to keep the centre of gravity 18 % ahead of the aerodynamic centre: the aircraft is already inherently unstable, and any mass added forward makes it worse. Use the modules slider: the speed goes up, and the checklist below turns red.

Raising the propeller efficiency was also tried. The activity-factor method puts the propeller at about 75 % efficiency, and the report finds a gain of 5 % in efficiency worth only 0.35 % in thrust, too little to matter, so changes to the propulsion system were set aside in favour of the wing.

Route 2: reduce the wing area

At the same 535 hp, a power per area of 84.8 hp/m² means a wing of 6.3 m², a reduction of 3.1 %. The span is held at 7.3 m to keep the ribs, spars and fittings the same, so the area is reduced by changing the chord:

$$S=\frac{(C_r+C_t)\,b}{2},\qquad \lambda=\frac{C_t}{C_r}$$

The report generated 15 planforms in MATLAB by varying the taper ratio and root chord at constant span and kept those with an area at or below 6.3 m². Delta wings, higher aspect ratios and forward-swept wings were excluded in advance: the delta cannot be trimmed at this speed because it would need about 69 % more lift than the aircraft can produce, forward sweep makes the aircraft unstable in roll (the rolling moment slope with sideslip turns positive), and swept-back wings cost induced drag at low subsonic speed and are a transonic device. Drag the root chord and taper sliders and watch the planform, the area and the speed respond.

Taper alone gave the best first change. It keeps the span, the main structure and the control-surface layout, and so it needs the least redesign and recertification. The selected concept has a root chord of about 1.19 m, a taper ratio of 0.45 and no sweep, which gives 6.28 m². Of the concepts that reduce the area enough, it was the only one that still satisfied the limit load factor required by the regulations (the wing becomes smaller and more heavily loaded as the area falls), and so the wing requirement fixed span, taper and a minimum area of 6.28 m². Press Report's wing to set it.

04 · REQUIREMENTS

The checklist, evaluated live

The user requirement specification turned the 1 % goal into numbers that a design either meets or does not. They are evaluated here for whatever the sliders are set to. The structural and aeroelastic parts of the design (the skin, spars, stringers and ribs of the new wing, and the divergence check) follow in the full report; the limit load factor of 3.61 g and the ultimate factor of 4.72 g are the structural targets those parts are designed against.

Overall

Why modify, not redesign. A cost-to-benefit comparison, with total cost the sum of development, production, operation and certification less any return, shows that for a small gain in speed a new design costs far more than the benefit, and a new design only pays when the gain is large, of the order of 40 %. For 1 %, a modified wing on a proven aircraft is the sensible answer.
